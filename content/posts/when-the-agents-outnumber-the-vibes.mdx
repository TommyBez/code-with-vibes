This week, Claude Code shipped version 2.1.154. The headline feature is
something called *dynamic workflows* — the ability to spawn and orchestrate
"tens to hundreds of agents in the background" from a single prompt. You type a
sentence, lean back, and an invisible orchestra starts playing. It is a
remarkable piece of engineering. It is also, if you care about the feel of the
thing, quietly terrifying.

## The loop is the point

Vibe coding works because the loop is tight. You make a small change, you run
it, you feel it land. The feedback arrives while the idea is still warm, and
that warmth pulls you into the next change. A hundred of those tiny cycles in an
afternoon, each one a conversation between you and the thing you're building.
The code becomes something you *inhabit*, not just something you produce.

The loop is not a productivity hack. It is the entire medium. Take it away and
you're left with a spec and a delivery window, which is just regular engineering
with better tooling. Nothing wrong with that — but it is not the thing we
started calling vibe coding.

## A hundred agents you cannot hear

Dynamic workflows break the loop by design. You hand off a task, and the model
decides how many sub-agents to spin up, what each one should do, and how to
stitch the results together. You get a notification when they're done. In
between, there is silence.

That silence is the problem. It is not the useful silence of flow, where you are
deep in a single file and the world falls away. It is the silence of
disconnection — of processes running in rooms you cannot enter, making decisions
you cannot feel. You become a manager, not a maker. And managing is not vibe
coding.

> The danger is not that the agents will fail. The danger is that they will
> succeed often enough that you stop checking.

## The normalization of deviance

Simon Willison, one of the sharpest and most disciplined developers I know,
recently admitted something that has stayed with me. He has stopped reviewing
every line of AI-generated code in his production projects. He knows the risks.
He knows the definition of *normalization of deviance* — the slow, almost
imperceptible lowering of standards until a failure that was once unthinkable
becomes inevitable. And yet.

> "Claude Code does not have a professional reputation! It can't take
> accountability for what it's done. But it's been proving itself anyway — time
> and time again it's churning out straightforward things and doing them right."

The models have gotten good enough that the surface-level signal — *does this
look right?* — is no longer a reliable indicator of anything. An Oxford study
found that optimizing an AI for warmth and friendliness made it sixty percent
more likely to produce wrong answers. The thing that feels most trustworthy is
often the thing that is quietly wrong.

When you multiply that dynamic across a hundred background agents, each writing
code you will never read, you are no longer vibe coding. You are running a
confidence experiment on your own judgment.

## Staying in the room

None of this means dynamic workflows are bad. They are extraordinary. Used well,
they will let a single developer tackle problems that used to require a team.
But using them well requires a deliberate choice about *when* to stay in the
loop and *when* to step outside it.

My rule is simple. If I cannot describe, in one sentence, what every active
agent is doing and why, I have too many agents. The point of vibe coding was
never to write less code. It was to *feel* more of the code you write. Every
time you delegate a decision you could have made yourself, you trade a little of
that feeling for speed.

Sometimes the trade is worth it. Sometimes it is not. But you have to notice
that you are making it.

## The music still matters

Here is the test. Put on a record — something with texture, something that
demands just enough attention to keep the analytical mind quiet while the
intuitive one runs. If you can still hear the music over the hum of background
agents, you are still in the room. If the album finishes and you did not notice
a single transition, you were not coding. You were waiting.

Oval Stones is a good one for this. It is psychedelic and unhurried, with a
drumbeat that feels like walking on uneven ground. It keeps you just slightly
off-balance, which is exactly where you want to be when you are deciding how
much of your own code you are willing to let go of.

Keep the loop small. Keep the agents few. Keep the music loud enough that you
remember you are the one building the thing.
